This semester my team and I worked with the Hendrix Advancement Office on what turned into one of the most real-world projects I've touched at Hendrix. The goal was to design and build a stand-alone website that showcases funding opportunities at Hendrix College, basically a curated database the advancement staff can pull up when they're sitting across from a prospective donor and trying to match that person's interests to something specific on campus.

Working under our community partner Dan Turner, here's what we got done:

- **Public-facing features:** project listing pages, individual project detail pages, and a clear call-to-action pointing donors toward the Advancement Office
- **Project data fields:** title, description, location, and the other details needed to actually pitch a funding opportunity
- **Admin interface** so staff can add, edit, and update projects without needing to touch the code
- **Submission feature** with a form where faculty or staff can propose a project (name, department, project description)
- **Hendrix orange styling** to keep the site visually consistent with the main .edu site
- **A solid SQL schema** behind the scenes, plus successful front-end and back-end development
- **Named spaces and student experience initiatives** covering rooms, buildings, outdoor areas, programs, and equipment

**What's still to come (whoever picks this up next, take notes):**
- Updated admin interface
- Secure login
- Better graphics and visual aids
- Cleaner instructions for donations

## What this project taught me about being a Product Manager intern

Honestly, this project was a real-time test of everything I've been doing at my Acxiom PM internship. Client communication with Dan Turner felt like a near-perfect mirror of how I've been talking to product owners and stakeholders at work. You can't just take a feature request at face value. You have to ask why, you have to ask who's actually going to use it, and you have to ask what happens if you don't build it. That's the job.

User stories were huge here. When Dan would describe what he needed, I'd find myself naturally translating it into the same "As a [user], I want [feature] so that [outcome]" format I've been using on Jira tickets at Acxiom. That habit made it way easier for me to hand off requirements to Jake, Chris, and Drew in a way that didn't leave them guessing. Simplifying complex client requests so my teammates could actually build against them was something I'd been practicing all summer and fall under Brandi and Kara, and this project let me prove the muscle actually works outside of work.

Skills that mattered most:
- Listening more than talking in client meetings
- Translating between non-technical stakeholders and developers
- Breaking big asks into smaller deliverables
- Following up when something is unclear instead of guessing

Where I came up short:
- I missed a client meeting and one presentation while my father was recovering from surgery. I owned it, but it stung. The real-world lesson there is that contingency planning matters as much as the work itself.

## The social change angle

The Advancement Office's mission sits in a part of higher ed people don't always frame as "social change" work, but it absolutely is. Every named space, every scholarship, every program at Hendrix exists because somebody decided to fund it. Without an organized way to show donors what they could be a part of, those opportunities stay invisible and the students who would benefit from them never feel it.

Dan's approach is effective because it meets donors where they are. Instead of a generic "give now" pitch, the site we built lets the Advancement Office say "here are real, specific opportunities, here's the impact, here's who to talk to." That's a more honest way to do philanthropy and it respects donors as partners instead of treating them as wallets.

## What got challenged and what got confirmed

My belief that good software is mostly a communication problem disguised as a technical problem got confirmed hard this semester. The hardest parts of this project weren't the SQL or the front-end work; they were making sure the client, the team, and the deliverable were all aligned. My Database & Web Systems coursework and my PM internship reinforced each other constantly. The CS classes gave me the vocabulary, and the internship gave me the muscle memory.

One thing I'd push back on: the assumption that being a strong individual contributor and being a strong PM use the same skill set. They don't. Knowing SQL is great. Knowing when not to write SQL and instead go ask the client a question is what actually moves a project forward.

## How this reshaped my view of the issues

I came into this thinking philanthropy was mostly about big donors and big gifts. What I learned is that the unsexy infrastructure piece, the database, the schema, the admin interface, is what makes the whole operation either work or stall out. If the back office can't quickly pull up "here are 12 projects under $50K aligned with this donor's interests," good intentions don't turn into actual dollars and actual student outcomes. The public issue I care more about now is how often nonprofit and mission-driven organizations are running on outdated systems, and how much impact gets left on the table because of it.

## Feedback from the Advancement Office

Dan and his team have been encouraging throughout, and the things they've wanted will be accounted for the next iteration (secure login, a more polished admin experience, clearer donation guidance, stronger visuals) are the right priorities. We built a working foundation. The next team gets to make it shine.

## Looking forward

Graduating this Saturday and moving to the next chapter of my life. I'm hunting data analyst and business analyst roles with a long-term goal of getting back to product management. This Odyssey project, plus Acxiom, plus the work I did leading this team, all stack up into a story I can actually tell in interviews. That's the part I didn't expect when I signed up for this class: the project ended up being one of the cleanest demonstrations of who I am professionally that I've ever put together.

Big thanks to Dan Turner and the Advancement Office for trusting four students with a real deliverable, to Dr. Goadrich for setting the whole thing up, and to Jake, Chris, and Drew for being the kind of teammates who made the work worth doing.
